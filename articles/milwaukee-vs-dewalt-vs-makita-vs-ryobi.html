Milwaukee vs DeWalt vs Makita vs Ryobi: Which Cordless Platform Should a Beginner Buy Into?

By the ThuKhai Team  ·  Platform Comparisons  ·  Updated October 2026

Affiliate disclosure: ThuKhai is not currently part of any affiliate program. There are no affiliate links on this page. See our full affiliate disclosure. This is a research-based buying guide — see how we test.

Quick answer: On a tight budget, buy Ryobi ONE+. Want one safe default that fits most homeowners, buy DeWalt 20V MAX. Serious about building a workshop over years, buy Milwaukee M18. Want the largest 18V lineup with a compact, refined feel, buy Makita LXT. The honest truth: all four are good. What differs is the price of getting in, the price of batteries later, and how deep each lineup goes.

Why this decision matters more than your first tool

Your first drill kit feels like a tool purchase. It is actually a subscription. Batteries are locked to one brand's mount — a DeWalt pack will never click into a Milwaukee tool — so the kit you buy this year decides what every future bare tool, battery, and charger in your home has to be. Our beginner's buying-order guide calls this the one-platform rule, and it is the single most expensive rule to break: switch brands later and you re-buy batteries and chargers from zero.

So do not compare first drills. Compare the four things you will actually live with: how much the lineup covers, what it costs to get in, what spare batteries cost, and how long the warranty lasts. That is what this page does, with US prices checked in October 2026.

DeWalt 20V MAX — the safe default

DeWalt is the platform most homeowners should default to, for boring practical reasons. It is sold at both Home Depot and Lowe's (the broadest shelf presence of the four), the lineup covers everything a house can ask for, and DeWalt adds two beginner-friendly safety nets nobody else matches: a 90-day money-back guarantee and a year of free service on top of the 3-year warranty.

The entry price is the second-lowest here: the DCD771C2 drill kit with two batteries runs $119 and drops to $99 on promo several times a year. One naming trap to ignore: "20V MAX" is the same 18-volt nominal battery everyone else sells — DeWalt prints the no-load maximum, and says so in its own footnotes. We explain the label trick in 12V vs 18V/20V; the short version is that it changes nothing about power.

DeWalt's one clever trick is FLEXVOLT: a FLEXVOLT pack runs your 20V MAX tools as a big 6.0 Ah battery and can also power DeWalt's 60V MAX outdoor and heavy tools. If a serious mower or a big saw is in your future, that crossover softens the step up.

DeWalt — pros

  • Lowest big-brand entry price; frequent $99 kit promos
  • Sold everywhere; 90-day money-back guarantee
  • FLEXVOLT packs bridge into 60V tools later

DeWalt — cons

  • Spare batteries are pricey at list price — buy them on promo
  • Entry kit batteries are small (1.3 Ah)
  • "20V MAX" label invites pointless comparisons

Buy DeWalt if: you want the default answer — a mainstream platform, fair prices, and a store nearby that stocks it.

Milwaukee M18 — for the serious DIYer

Milwaukee is the platform people graduate to, and plenty of beginners should simply start there. M18 tools — especially the brushless FUEL tier — are built for daily professional use, and Milwaukee backs that claim with the best tool warranty of the four: 5 years. If you expect to still be building things in ten years, that warranty and the deep pro lineup are worth paying for.

You will pay for it. The entry drill kit is $199 where DeWalt asks $119, and Milwaukee's spare 5.0 Ah two-pack lists at $269 — the highest here, though it does appear near $134 in seasonal promos. Milwaukee is also effectively a Home Depot brand at retail in the US (plus pro dealers like Acme Tools).

One structural note: Milwaukee runs a second full platform, the compact M12 line, alongside M18. Most Milwaukee kit chargers charge both, which is genuinely handy — but M12 and M18 batteries do not fit each other's tools, so adding M12 later means a second battery family, even if it shares your charger.

Milwaukee — pros

  • 5-year tool warranty — best of the four
  • FUEL brushless tier is the performance benchmark
  • Huge pro-grade lineup; M12 compact line shares chargers

Milwaukee — cons

  • Highest entry price and highest battery list prices
  • Compact CP batteries carry only a 2-year warranty
  • Retail is mostly Home Depot

Buy Milwaukee if: you are building a real workshop over years and would rather pay once for the top tier.

Makita LXT 18V — the largest 18V lineup, the refined feel

Makita created the 18V lithium-ion category in 2005 and has run one battery interface ever since — no forced re-buys, which is exactly the promise a beginner wants. The LXT system is now, by Makita's own claim, the world's largest compatible 18V slide-style system at 350+ products, and the tools have a reputation for compact size and good balance that the spec sheet never shows.

Makita's beginner pricing works differently: instead of a cheap drill-only kit, the sensible entries are combos — a drill + impact driver kit at $199, or the brushless XT281S combo at $269 with bigger 3.0 Ah batteries. Spare 5.0 Ah two-packs typically land around $175–200. Charging is a Makita party trick: a 5.0 Ah pack refills in about 45 minutes. And Makita's 36V tools simply run on two of your standard LXT batteries — no new battery family needed.

The caveat is the step-up line: Makita's 40V max XGT system is completely separate and does not share batteries with LXT in either direction. Treat XGT as a different brand you might buy someday, not an upgrade path.

Makita — pros

  • Largest 18V lineup; one battery interface since 2005
  • Compact, well-balanced tools; fast charging (5.0 Ah in ~45 min)
  • 36V tools run on two standard LXT packs

Makita — cons

  • No truly cheap drill-only starter kit
  • Spare batteries rarely see Milwaukee/DeWalt-level promo prices
  • 40V XGT is a separate system — not an upgrade path

Buy Makita if: you value how tools feel in the hand, want the deepest 18V catalog, and like the idea of never re-buying batteries.

Ryobi ONE+ 18V — the budget champion

Ryobi is the beginner platform in the purest sense: the cheapest way in, and the widest net. A drill + impact combo kit with two batteries is $149, drill-only kits run $99–139, and the recurring $99 promo for a 4.0 Ah two-pack with charger (often with a free bare tool) is the best battery deal in this comparison by a wide margin.

The ONE+ lineup's 300+ products wander far beyond the workshop — inflators, fans, scrubbers, lights, cleaning gear, yard tools — all on the same battery. Ryobi also has the best backward-compatibility story here: the platform dates to 1996, and today's lithium packs still run the old blue NiCd-era tools. If you inherit or find used Ryobi tools, your new batteries will very likely fit.

The trade-offs are real but honest: Ryobi tools are built for home use, not daily jobsite abuse, and in the US the brand is sold almost exclusively through Home Depot. Also mind the yard aisle: Ryobi's 40V outdoor line is a separate system from ONE+ 18V, so it is easy to accidentally start two battery collections. (Ryobi's tiny USB Lithium tools are a third mini-line for small household jobs — fun, separate, cheap.)

Ryobi — pros

  • Cheapest kits and cheapest batteries of the four
  • 300+ products, including lifestyle and yard tools
  • New batteries still fit tools back to 1996

Ryobi — cons

  • Homeowner-grade durability, not pro-grade
  • Sold almost only at Home Depot in the US
  • 40V yard line is a separate battery system

Buy Ryobi if: money is the main constraint, or your projects are as much about the house and yard as the workshop. It is the platform we point first-time buyers to when the budget is under ~$200 all-in.

The cost nobody shows you: batteries

Every entry kit above ships with small batteries — 1.3 to 1.5 Ah compacts. They are fine for drills and drivers, and genuinely inadequate for circular saws and grinders, which want 4.0 Ah packs or bigger. So the real price of a platform is the kit plus the big batteries you will buy within a year. Rerun the table with that lens and the ranking sharpens: Ryobi's ~$99 battery promos make it the cheapest system to grow, DeWalt and Milwaukee reward patient promo-watching, and Makita costs a steady mid-price throughout.

The good news: once you own two decent batteries and a charger, growth gets cheap. A bare tool typically costs about a third less than the same tool in kit form — which is the entire economic point of choosing one platform and staying on it, as our buying-order guide lays out.

The second-platform trap, in one minute

Each brand will happily sell you a second battery family. Know which ones share before you buy:

  • DeWalt FLEXVOLT (60V): shares — FLEXVOLT packs also run your 20V MAX tools. Safe to add later.
  • Milwaukee M12: half-shares — chargers charge both M12 and M18, but the batteries do not cross. A deliberate second system.
  • Makita XGT 40V: fully separate — no battery crosses in either direction.
  • Ryobi 40V: fully separate — your ONE+ 18V packs will not run 40V yard tools.

None of this should scare you off a brand. It should stop you from buying a "bargain" second system by accident in the garden aisle.

So, which one should you buy?

Budget first, or first apartment? Ryobi ONE+. The savings are real, the lineup is enormous, and you can sell out and switch later having lost the least.

Average homeowner who wants one safe answer? DeWalt 20V MAX. Fair prices, sold everywhere, and a money-back guarantee while you decide.

Planning a decade of projects? Milwaukee M18. The highest entry price buys the strongest tools and the longest warranty.

Care about feel, balance, and the biggest 18V catalog? Makita LXT. The connoisseur's pick, with twenty years of one battery to show for it.

And if you are still torn between two: buy the one whose batteries go on sale at the store you actually shop at. You will be buying batteries for a decade — make them cheap and convenient to get.

FAQ

Can I use one brand's batteries on another brand's tools?

No. All four mounts are physically different, and no brand supports cross-use. Third-party adapters exist but are not manufacturer-supported — we do not recommend building a beginner setup on them.

Is DeWalt's 20V MAX more powerful than the others' 18V?

No — same five cells, same 18V nominal working voltage; "20V" is the no-load maximum. See 12V vs 18V/20V for the full explanation.

Which platform is cheapest to expand later?

Ryobi, and it is not close — the cheapest bare tools and the cheapest battery promos. DeWalt is second if you buy batteries on sale.

Which has the best warranty?

Milwaukee, on tools: 5 years versus 3 for the other three. Batteries are 3 years nearly everywhere (Milwaukee's compact CP packs are the exception at 2 years).

Should I wait for a sale before buying in?

If you can wait a few weeks, yes. All four brands run aggressive promotions from October through December, and kit and battery prices in the table above already reflect typical promo levels.

Bottom line

There is no wrong door here — there are only doors that cost more to walk through. Ryobi is the cheapest, DeWalt the safest, Milwaukee the strongest, Makita the deepest. Pick the one that matches your budget and your ambitions, buy the kit with two batteries, and then stop shopping for platforms: every tool after the first one is where the savings live.
