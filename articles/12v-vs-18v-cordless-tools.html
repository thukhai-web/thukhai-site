12V vs 18V/20V Cordless Tools: Which Battery Platform Is Right for DIY?

By the ThuKhai Team  ·  Beginner Guides  ·  Updated October 2026

Affiliate disclosure: ThuKhai is not currently part of any affiliate program. There are no affiliate links on this page. See our full affiliate disclosure. This is a research-based buying guide — see how we test.

Quick answer: Start with 18V/20V — it is the default platform that handles everything a beginner will attempt. Choose 12V only if your work is genuinely light-duty and you want lighter, cheaper tools, or as a second system later. And stop worrying about 18V vs 20V: those two numbers describe the same battery.

First, kill the 18V vs 20V confusion

This is the most common beginner trap, so let us clear it before anything else: a "20V MAX" battery and an "18V" battery are the same thing.

Both hold the same five lithium-ion cells wired in series. Each cell delivers about 3.6 volts while working — 5 × 3.6 V = 18 volts nominal, the voltage you actually get while drilling. Measure those same cells fresh off the charger, with no load, and each reads closer to 4.0 volts — 5 × 4.0 V = 20 volts maximum.

DeWalt prints the maximum number ("20V MAX") under US labeling conventions; Milwaukee, Makita, and Bosch print the nominal number ("18V"), which is what European rules require. DeWalt says so itself — the footnote on its battery pages reads: "Maximum initial battery voltage (measured without a workload) is 20 volts. Nominal voltage is 18." Same cells, same power, same runtime. The difference is marketing, not engineering.

Two things this does not mean: it does not make a 20V MAX tool stronger than an 18V tool — compare the tools and their ecosystems, never the voltage label. And it does not make batteries interchangeable: a DeWalt 20V MAX battery only fits DeWalt 20V MAX tools, because the mounts differ by brand.

What voltage actually changes: 12V vs 18V/20V

The real choice is between the 12-volt class and the 18-volt class. Here is what changes:

12V systems18V/20V systems
Weight and sizeNoticeably lighter and more compact; batteries often slide into the handle itselfHeavier; batteries mount on a rail under the tool
PowerPlenty for drilling and driving in wood, drywall, and plasticAll of that, plus saws, grinders, and masonry drilling
Runtime per batteryShorter on hard work; fine for quick jobsLonger; high-draw tools want 4.0 Ah packs or bigger
Tool rangeCompact but narrower lineupsThe deepest ecosystems — hundreds of tools on one battery
Cost to startLowerHigher
Best forLight-duty users, tight spaces, overhead workThe one-platform-does-everything default

Who should buy 12V

Twelve-volt tools are genuinely good now — early ones were underpowered, but modern 12V drills and drivers handle real household work. Buy 12V if most of this describes you:

  • Your work is light: assembling furniture, hanging pictures and curtain rods, mounting shelves on drywall or wood studs, driving everyday screws, small repairs, hobby electronics.
  • You work overhead or inside cabinets a lot: a lighter tool you can hold with one hand for ten minutes beats a stronger one you have to put down after two. Every ounce matters on a ladder.
  • You want the cheapest way in: 12V starter kits cost less, so they are a low-risk way to find out whether you will actually use cordless tools regularly.
  • As a second system: you already own 18V tools and want a featherweight drill or driver for quick jobs where the big kit is overkill.

The 12V lineups worth knowing are Milwaukee M12, DeWalt 12V MAX, Makita 12Vmax CXT, and Bosch 12V. Milwaukee's M12 is the standout example of the class done right: the battery slides into the tool's handle, so the tool stays slim and balanced instead of carrying a bulky pack underneath.

The honest limit: 12V will not drive many deck screws, will not run a circular saw through plywood all afternoon, and will not drill into brick. If any of that is on your project list, 12V is the wrong first platform.

Who should buy 18V/20V — most beginners start here

For most beginners, 18V/20V is the default first platform, and it is the one we recommend. It does everything 12V does, plus the jobs that turn a homeowner into a builder:

  • Cutting lumber and plywood with a cordless circular saw
  • Cutting and grinding metal with an angle grinder
  • Drilling into brick and masonry with a hammer drill
  • Driving long deck screws and lag bolts without stalling

The ecosystems are also the deepest: Ryobi ONE+ 18V runs hundreds of tools on one battery, and DeWalt 20V MAX, Milwaukee M18, and Makita 18V LXT each cover everything from drills to lawn equipment. Once you own the batteries and charger, every additional bare tool is comparatively cheap — that is the whole economic argument for picking one platform and staying on it.

The cost trap to avoid: buying 12V first because it is cheaper, then upgrading to 18V/20V six months later when a real project arrives. Two platforms, two chargers, two sets of batteries — that costs more than starting on 18V/20V once. If you might ever cut a board or drill a brick wall, start here.

The voltage is not the decision — the platform is

Here is the decision that actually matters, and it is not a number: a DeWalt battery will never fit a Milwaukee tool. Batteries are locked to their brand's mount, so your first kit purchase is really a vote for a battery family you will live with for years. Our buying-order guide calls this the one-platform rule: buy every later tool as a bare tool on the same system.

So the real question is not "12 or 18?" but "which brand's 18V family do I want to live in?" That is the brand comparison — Milwaukee vs DeWalt vs Makita vs Ryobi — and it is the next guide in this series. Read this voltage guide first so the brand comparison makes sense, then make the platform decision once and stop thinking about it.

Amp-hours matter more than volts for runtime

One last battery lesson, because it saves beginners real money: voltage is the power class, amp-hours (Ah) are the fuel tank. A 2.0 Ah battery is fine for a drill and an impact driver. Saws, grinders, and sanders pull far more current and want 4.0 Ah or bigger — a small battery will drain in minutes on a circular saw. Two batteries remains the practical minimum: one in the tool, one on the charger. When a kit gives you a choice, take the kit with the larger batteries.

FAQ

Is 20V more powerful than 18V?

No. Same five cells, same 18V nominal working voltage — the "20V" is the no-load maximum printed for marketing. Compare brands and tools, not the voltage label.

Can I use a 12V battery on an 18V tool?

No. Different voltage and a physically different mount — it will not fit, and forcing it is a good way to damage the tool or the battery. Voltage and brand must both match.

Can a 12V drill handle concrete or brick?

Not really. Masonry wants an 18V/20V hammer drill at minimum (or an SDS rotary hammer for serious concrete). A 12V drill will stall and overheat long before the hole is done.

Should a beginner own both 12V and 18V?

Start with one — 18V/20V for most people. Most beginners never need the second system. Add 12V later only if you find yourself wishing your drill were lighter for quick jobs.

What about 36V, 40V, or 60V tools?

Those are pro-grade systems for continuous heavy work — big saws, grinders, mowers, and outdoor equipment. Overkill in both power and price for a beginner's first kit.

Bottom line

Buy 18V/20V first, one brand, as a kit with two batteries. Buy 12V instead — or later, as a second system — only if your work is genuinely light and you value light weight over versatility. And ignore the 18V-vs-20V number war completely: it was never real.
